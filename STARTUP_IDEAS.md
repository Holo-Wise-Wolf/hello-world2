**Compiled October 2026.** Every idea below was tested against live 2025–2026 reimbursement rules, regulatory changes, funding mechanisms, and the existing competitive field. Ideas that were researched and *rejected* are listed at the end, with reasons — the rejections are as useful as the picks.

## 1. Billable community health navigation — the single best risk-adjusted bet

**Weighted score: 8.75**

### The opening

In 2024 Medicare created two new families of HCPCS codes that almost nobody is using:

- **Community Health Integration (CHI):** `G0019` (first 60 min/month) and `G0022` (each additional 30 min)
- **Principal Illness Navigation (PIN):** `G0023` / `G0024`, plus peer-support variants `G0140` / `G0146`

Reported non-facility rates run roughly **$79/patient/month for G0019 and ~$49 for each G0022 increment** ([Medical Economics, Apr 2026](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/new-medicare-codes-could-transform-how-physicians-serve-their-most-vulnerable-patients-here-s-how-to-use-them)) — confirm against the current CMS fee schedule before modeling.

Three facts make this a genuine opening rather than a crowded market:

1. **The work can be done by "auxiliary personnel" under *general* supervision.** The billing physician does not need to be present ([Medical Economics](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/new-medicare-codes-could-transform-how-physicians-serve-their-most-vulnerable-patients-here-s-how-to-use-them); [Noridian CHI guidance](https://med.noridianmedicare.com/web/jfb/specialties/care-coordination/community-health-integration-chi-services)). A trained, certified community health worker — which an undergraduate can become — is a *billable clinical asset*.
2. **Adoption is thin.** Per the most recent CMS data, national uptake of the navigation codes in 2024 was limited ([CodingIntel](https://codingintel.com/community-health-integration-chi-services/)). The money is appropriated and sitting unused because clinics lack the staffing model to capture it.
3. **2026 widened the door.** Effective 1/1/2026, psychiatric diagnostic evaluation (CPT 90791) and Health Behavior Assessment and Intervention (96156–96168) now qualify as initiating visits, and CMS broadened the language from "social determinants" to **"upstream drivers"** — a deliberately broader interpretation of what counts as a billable barrier ([CodingIntel](https://codingintel.com/community-health-integration-chi-services/)).

### Why this is not already taken

Solace Health raised a **$130M Series C at a >$1B valuation in February 2026** on advocacy for Medicare members ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/solace-health-raises-130m-bringing-it-unicorn-status)) — proof the category is real and fundable. But Solace sells *to patients* with a network of 2,000+ experienced advocates. Nobody is building the **low-cost, supervised, clinic-contracted layer** for FQHCs, rural clinics, and small practices that can't hire a full-time CHW but can bill $79/month across 150 patients.

### The precedent that should make you confident

**Health Leads.** Rebecca Onie co-founded it as a **Harvard sophomore in 1996** with Dr. Barry Zuckerman at Boston Medical Center, mobilizing college students to connect low-income families with food, housing, and childcare through clinic "prescriptions." It scaled to dozens of clinical sites, a 2016 JAMA Internal Medicine study tied the model to improved blood pressure and cholesterol, and Onie received a **MacArthur Fellowship in 2009** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_Leads); [MacArthur Foundation](https://www.macfound.org/fellows/class-of-2009/rebecca-onie); [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/medicaid/health-leads/)).

Health Leads ran on grants. **You would run the same model on a Medicare billing code that didn't exist in 1996.** That's the whole thesis.

### Business model

Contract with 2–3 clinics. You recruit, train, and certify the navigators (your campus is the labor pool); the clinic bills CHI/PIN under its practitioner's general supervision; you take a per-member-per-month fee out of the collected revenue. At $79/month against a PMPM fee of $35–45 across 300 patients, you're at **$125k–160k ARR** with no outside capital.

### Moat

Thin at first (it's a services business), thickening three ways: (a) the **compliance artifact** — documented initiating visits, consent, time logs, and medical-necessity evidence that survives a Noridian audit is genuinely hard and is what clinics are actually scared of; (b) **certification pipeline** — state CHW certification requirements vary, and an ops layer that handles them across states is defensible; (c) **outcomes data** — once you have 12 months of utilization/readmission data tied to navigation hours, you can sell to Medicaid MCOs and move to risk.

### Risks, honestly

- **Rate risk.** A single Physician Fee Schedule rule could cut the rates. Mitigate by diversifying into state Medicaid CHW benefits early — Washington's Apple Health CHW billing guide took effect July 1, 2026, and Minnesota has a CHI workflow in place ([WA HCA](https://www.hca.wa.gov/assets/billers-and-providers/community-health-workers-bg-20260701.pdf)).
- **You are not the biller.** Your revenue depends on the clinic's billing competence. Pick partners with a functioning RCM operation, or own that function.
- **Thin adoption cuts both ways** — it may mean the codes are operationally painful, not just unknown. Validate by talking to three billing managers before writing code.

### First 90 days

1. Get certified as a CHW in your state (most programs are 60–120 hours).
2. Interview 10 clinic administrators: ask specifically "have you tried to bill G0019, and what stopped you?"
3. Build the documentation template, not an app. The product is an audit-proof workflow.
4. Run 20 patients at one clinic, free, to prove the time logs clear.

## 2. Indigenous-language medical interpreting + the speech corpus nobody owns

**Weighted score: 9.00 — highest-scoring idea on the list**

### The opening

**Mam, K'iche', and Q'anjob'al have each become one of the 25 most common languages in U.S. immigration court** ([NYT, via All Things Linguistic](https://allthingslinguistic.com/tagged/mam)). UCSF Benioff Children's Hospital Oakland alone fields **over 1,000 Mam interpreter requests per year**, and tele-interpretation routinely fails because almost no staff speak it ([UCSF](https://pediatrics.ucsf.edu/news/breaking-down-barriers-vital-role-mam-speaking-healthcare-interpreters)).

Meanwhile, the regulatory floor just got firmer. Under the **Section 1557 final rule (effective July 5, 2024)**, covered entities may use machine translation — but a **qualified human translator must review the output** in defined high-stakes situations ([Dynamic Language](https://blog.dynamiclanguage.com/the-machine-translation-guardrail-in-section-1557-proving-which-content-a-human-reviewed)). Hospitals must also be able to *prove* which content a human reviewed, and most cannot.

### Why machine translation will not eat this

The general-purpose models are not coming for Mam. The evidence is unambiguous: a 2025 benchmark of isiXhosa primary-care consultations found pretrained speech recognition models produced **character error rates of 26–70%** ([CL4Health 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2025.cl4health-1.14)). Mayan languages have *less* digital text than isiXhosa. Commercial medical translation startups confirm the gap by omission — **No Barrier** ($2.7M seed) covers "40+ languages" that are Spanish, Mandarin, Arabic and similar ([Healthcare Brew](https://www.healthcare-brew.com/stories/2025/12/15/startup-no-barrier-ai-improve-medical-translation)); none of them touch Mam or Mixtec.

**This is the rarest thing in AI right now: a problem where the data does not exist and cannot be scraped.** Whoever builds the corpus owns the category.

### The business

Two-layer company:

- **Layer 1 (revenue now):** A specialist interpreter network. Recruit, train, and credential Mam/K'iche'/Mixtec/Q'anjob'al speakers — largely from the diaspora communities themselves — and sell on-demand video interpreting to hospitals, FQHCs, and school districts at a premium rate. Hospitals already pay for rare-language interpreting and currently just fail to get it.
- **Layer 2 (the moat):** Every consented, de-identified encounter becomes training data. Within two years you hold the only medical speech corpus for these languages. That asset is licensable, publishable, and essentially unreproducible by a competitor who starts later.

There's a working template for the research half: a Gates Grand Challenges grant currently funds an Amharic/Afan Oromo/Somali health speech-to-speech system built by fine-tuning on anonymized doctor-patient audio ([Grand Challenges](https://gcgh.grandchallenges.org/grant/ethiopian-languages-speech-speech-translation-system-health-sector-using-large-language)). You'd be doing the U.S. version with a revenue engine attached.

### Why this is extraordinary on a med school application

It is simultaneously: a health equity intervention with a measurable access outcome, an original dataset and likely a first-author paper, a linguistics/CS contribution, and direct sustained contact with a patient population almost no applicant has worked with. It answers "tell me about a time you saw a gap in care" with something no admissions committee has heard before.

### Risks

- **Recruiting interpreters is the hard part**, not the technology. Mam speakers in the U.S. are concentrated (Oakland, D.C., parts of Florida and Georgia) and community trust must be earned, not bought. Start where you have a real relationship.
- **Consent and data governance.** You are collecting medical audio from a vulnerable, often undocumented population. Get IRB involvement early even though a company doesn't strictly need it. Do this wrong and the whole thing is indefensible — ethically and commercially.
- Dialect fragmentation is real: "Mixtec" covers **50+ varieties** ([Certified Languages](https://certifiedlanguages.com/blog/over-500-indigenous-languages-spoken-in-latin-america/)). Pick one language and one metro to start.

## 3. Medical-frailty exemption infrastructure

**Weighted score: 7.95 — highest urgency, shortest window**

### The opening

Medicaid community engagement (work) requirements take effect **January 1, 2027**, under CMS's interim final rule **CMS-2454-IFC issued June 1, 2026** ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/medicaid-community-engagement-requirement-certain-individuals-interim-final-rule-comment-period-cms)). Roughly 43–44 jurisdictions are affected; Nebraska started May 1, 2026, Iowa is set for December 1, 2026 ([CHCS](https://www.chcs.org/resource/a-summary-of-national-medicaid-work-requirements/); [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/an-early-look-at-policy-decisions-as-states-get-ready-to-implement-work-requirements/)).

The leverage point is the **medical frailty exemption**, and CMS defined it narrowly. States must first attempt verification using claims and encounter data from the preceding 12 months; **self-attestation is permitted through 2027, usable only once starting in 2028**, after which enrollees must submit documentation proving their health status ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/the-medical-frailty-exemption-from-medicaid-work-requirements-key-takeaways-from-the-cms-interim-final-rule/)).

Critically: the rule ties frailty to whether a condition **impairs the individual's ability to meet the community engagement requirement** — not merely to a diagnosis category. That is a clinical judgment, and it lands on providers.

### Who is going to do this work, and why it's a business

Providers will be asked to certify medical frailty, verify temporary inability to work after hospitalization, and participate in appeals — **work that is unlikely to generate any reimbursement** and that will fall hardest on safety-net and rural providers ([AMA issue brief](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/issue-brief-medicaid-work-requirements-medical-frailty-exemption.pdf)). The American College of Physicians is already telling members to build **standardized EHR templates for exemption documentation** ([ACP](https://www.acponline.org/sites/default/files/documents/advocacy/state_health_policy/how-can-physicians-prepare-for-new-medicaid-requirements.pdf)).

Nobody sells that template. Fortuna Health raised **$18M from a16z** for Medicaid enrollment and recertification ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/fortuna-health-raises-18m-enhance-ai-medicaid-enrollment)) — adjacent, but enrollment ≠ clinical exemption documentation. States are mostly reusing incumbent eligibility vendors because new RFPs can't close in time ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/an-early-look-at-policy-decisions-as-states-get-ready-to-implement-work-requirements/)), which means the *provider-side* tooling is unbuilt.

### Product

Start as a free, open clinical toolkit — a frailty-criteria crosswalk mapping CMS's statutory categories and functional-impairment test to structured EHR fields (problem list codes, functional status, claims history) — plus a one-page generated attestation letter. Distribute through state primary care associations and FQHC networks. Monetize later as a per-clinic subscription or a managed service that does the documentation for clinics.

### Risks — read these before committing

- **Political volatility is extreme.** The requirements could be delayed, enjoined, or materially rewritten. Build nothing that only works under the exact current rule.
- **Short window.** The acute pain is 2027–2028. This is a 24-month opportunity, not a decade-long one — which caps the moat score.
- **Ethical complexity.** You are building tooling inside a policy that is projected to cause coverage losses. The defensible position is clear: you help eligible people keep coverage they are legally entitled to. Stay on that side of the line and the story is strong; drift toward helping states disenroll people and it's indefensible.

## 4. Clinical research coordinator pipeline

**Weighted score: 7.80 — most reliably profitable, least glamorous**

### The opening

The CRC shortage is structural, not cyclical. An Advarra workforce survey found **nearly 1 in 2 respondents reporting current staffing shortages**, with **CRC roles carrying the most vacancies** — more than nurses or PIs — and only 10–15% expecting improvement within five years ([Advarra](https://resource.advarra.com/hubfs/eBook/Advarra%20Workforce%20Development%20Survey%20Report%20EB.pdf)). One analysis cites **7 job postings per experienced coordinator seeking work**. Resignations among experienced staff ran **60% higher in 2022 than 2020**. SCRS reports the shortage is causing studies to be delayed and **some sites to decline new studies outright** ([SCRS](https://myscrs.org/resources/sites-now/exploring-the-current-clinical-workforce/)).

Simultaneously, the traditional pre-med clinical job is evaporating: medical scribe headcount fell from roughly **100,000 in 2020 to about 50,000 in 2024** as ambient AI took over documentation, with job postings down ~20% in 2025 ([Scribeberry analysis](https://scribeberry.com/guides/will-ai-replace-medical-scribes)).

**One market has a desperate labor shortage. An adjacent market just lost its primary entry-level role.** Nobody has connected them.

### Why the obvious objection is wrong

"Undergrads can't be coordinators." Correct — they can't be *lead* coordinators. But the CRC role decomposes: regulatory binder maintenance, source document prep, pre-screening chart review, participant scheduling and retention calls, data entry and query resolution. Those are trainable in weeks and are exactly what's drowning senior coordinators. You're selling **CRC-assistant capacity**, which lets a site's experienced coordinator run more studies.

The competitive field is thin and none of it targets students: **UpTrials** is a marketplace with only ~$205k raised ([Caplight](https://www.caplight.com/company/uptrials)); **Circuit Clinical** does virtual coordinator training within its own network; **RapidTrials** and Beacon Hill are conventional staffing firms placing experienced people.

### Business model

Train cohorts (GCP, HIPAA, protocol basics), place them at academic and community research sites at an hourly bill rate, pay the students, keep the spread. This is the **cash engine** of the list — real revenue in months, no regulatory approval, no capital. Standard staffing gross margins are 25–40%.

### Moat and the honest limitation

Moat comes from the credentialed talent pool and site relationships, which compound. But let's be clear: **this is a staffing company**, which is why it scores 6 on novelty. Its job is to fund something more ambitious, and to put you — and dozens of peers — inside real trials. The med school value is unusually concrete: you'd be the person who placed 50 pre-meds into research positions.

## 5. De novo protein binder foundry for orphan reagents

**Weighted score: 8.35 — the "difficulty ≠ impossibility" pick**

### The opening you're probably underestimating

Three things became true simultaneously, and most people haven't noticed the combination:

1. **Design is free.** RFdiffusion and BindCraft are open and run on a single GPU.
2. **Wet-lab validation is a credit-card purchase.** Adaptyv Bio tests a designed binder for **~$149 per protein including two replicates**, with an "Adaptyv Prime" 3-day turnaround in private beta as of September 2026 ([Adaptyv/aiwiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/adaptyv_bio)). You can test 30 designs for under $5,000.
3. **The approach demonstrably works, including for non-experts.** In Adaptyv's EGFR Round 2, **130 designers submitted 1,131 binders** and the winner hit **1.21 nM affinity** ([Cradle](https://cradle.bio/blog/adaptyv2)). A Nipah competition (Oct 2025–Jan 2026) saw a **9.3% hit rate across 1,200 tested designs with a best affinity of 0.37 nM**.

An undergraduate in 2026 can design, order, and validate a functional protein binder for less than the cost of a used car. That was a well-funded lab's project in 2019.

### Where the money is: don't compete with Monod Bio on diagnostics

Monod Bio (Baker lab spinout) is commercializing designed binders — its NovoBody/NovoLISA platform won a **$750k Gates Foundation grant** for a point-of-care preeclampsia test ([CLP](https://clpmag.com/diagnostic-technologies/point-of-care/poc-rapid-assays/ai-designed-proteins-target-preeclampsia-diagnosis-point-care-test/)). Don't fight that.

Go where incumbents won't: **orphan research-use-only reagents.** Thousands of targets have no good commercial antibody — obscure post-translational modifications, non-model-organism proteins, veterinary and agricultural antigens, environmental toxins. Academic labs waste months and tens of thousands of dollars on antibodies that don't work. RUO reagents require **no FDA submission**, so you can sell immediately.

The thermostability advantage is documented: designed binders survive conditions that destroy antibodies, and an Oxford group showed non-antibody binders matched antibodies in SARS-CoV-2 lateral flow tests with **better shelf life because they refold** ([ProImmune](https://www.proimmune.com/mi2025-adam-creamer)). For field diagnostics and low-resource settings, that's decisive.

### Tailwind

The FDA is actively phasing out animal testing requirements — a March 2026 draft guidance set out criteria for qualifying New Approach Methodologies, the agency has qualified an AI tool for in silico models, and it launched a searchable database of acceptable alternatives ([Hogan Lovells](https://insights.hoganlovells.com/report/medtech-forum-2026/congress-fda-advance-policies-to-phase-out-animal-testing); [Pharmaceutical Technology](https://www.pharmtech.com/view/fda-reaches-key-goals-for-reduction-of-animal-testing-in-drug-development)). Animal-derived antibodies are on the wrong side of this trend. Non-animal binders are on the right side.

### Risks

- **You need wet-lab access** for anything past Adaptyv's screening. Solve this with a faculty sponsor — frame it as a collaboration, not a favor.
- **Expression and scale-up is the real engineering problem**, not design. Budget for failure here.
- Highest technical risk on the list. Also the highest ceiling, and the one that gets most interesting over the next decade.

## 6. Penicillin allergy de-labeling as a service

**Weighted score: 8.20**

### The opening

Roughly **90% of hospitalized patients carrying a penicillin allergy label do not have a true allergy**. The consequence is concrete: a retrospective cohort of **8,385 surgical patients found a 50% increased risk of surgical site infection** from the alternative prophylaxis those labels force ([MN Dept. of Health](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/onehealthabx/event/may53ba.pdf)).

The clinical pathway is solved and validated. **PEN-FAST** risk-stratifies in minutes; low-risk patients get a direct oral amoxicillin challenge. Houston Methodist embedded the PEN-FAST questionnaire **directly into the EHR admission navigator for nursing staff system-wide** ([Houston Methodist](https://scholars.houstonmethodist.org/en/publications/distribution-of-pen-fast-scores-across-a-large-health-system-an-o/)). A Canadian tertiary center ran a pharmacist-led program and noted it previously **had no standardized approach at all** ([PMC12912928](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12912928/)).

**My searches found no commercial vendor selling this.** A validated protocol, a clear clinical benefit, hundreds of hospitals without a program, and no product. That's unusual.

### Be honest about the economics

The evidence is genuinely mixed and you should lead with that, not hide it. A 2025 UK economic evaluation found **no short-term cost savings** from proactive de-labeling, with cost per de-labeled patient from £577 (haematology-oncology) to £2,329 (acute medical) under a nurse-led model. Earlier modeling reached the opposite conclusion — a Portuguese analysis found testing cost-saving in all models (~$657 inpatient savings), and a Minnesota program cites **~$1,444 per average U.S. inpatient**.

The reconciliation: savings depend heavily on *which patients* you screen. **Target the high-yield populations** — pre-surgical patients (where the 50% SSI signal lives), oncology, and ICU — not everyone. A general screen loses money; a targeted one doesn't.

### Model

Sell a managed program to community hospitals without allergy/immunology coverage: the PEN-FAST EHR workflow, trained screeners (supervised by the hospital's pharmacist), the oral challenge protocol, and outcome reporting tied to antimicrobial stewardship metrics hospitals already report. Antimicrobial stewardship is a Joint Commission requirement — you're helping them meet an existing obligation.

### Med school value

Near-perfect. You'd be running a real antimicrobial stewardship intervention with publishable before/after data (de-labeling rates, cefazolin use, SSI rates), working alongside pharmacists and infectious disease physicians. Prior QI projects of exactly this shape have been published as conference abstracts — the bar for contribution is reachable.

## 7. Delirium-prevention care companions

**Weighted score: 7.85**

### The evidence is unusually strong

The Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) is one of the best-evidenced non-pharmacological interventions in hospital medicine. Hshieh et al. (2018, *Am J Geriatric Psychiatry*) pooled 14 studies: **delirium incidence OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.37–0.59)** and **falls OR 0.58 (95% CI 0.35–0.95)**. The earlier 2015 *JAMA Internal Medicine* analysis of multicomponent interventions across 4,267 patients found delirium **reduced by 44%** and falls by **64%** in randomized/matched trials. Cost savings: **$1,600–$3,800 per patient** in 2018 dollars ([ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/e108b116-9454-ed11-813d-005056834e2b)).

**And the model runs on trained volunteers.** The reviews explicitly identify the volunteer role as valuable across studies.

### The buying trigger: sitter spend

Hospitals burn money on 1:1 patient sitters and hate it. Reported figures: **~$2.1M/year** at a facility averaging 21 sitters per shift; **$3M/year unbudgeted** at a 900-bed hospital. Virtual sitting benchmarks the alternative at **$3.05/hour versus ~$26/hour** for in-person 1:1 sitters, and VA North Texas reported **$3.4M annual savings** from virtual sitters.

Sell to the CNO on **sitter-hour displacement**, with delirium and fall reduction as the clinical proof. One HELP program evaluation documented sitter hours falling from 4,210 to 3,743 and falls from 15 to 9, for **~$48k minimum savings on a single nursing unit** — though note the sitter reduction was not statistically significant (p=0.80).

### Honest assessment

**Lowest moat on the list (5).** It's a people business with no technology barrier; a hospital can copy it in-house. Two mitigations: (a) build the measurement layer — automated delirium screening (CAM) capture, sitter-hour attribution, and fall tracking is the part hospitals can't easily replicate; (b) credential your companions under CHW certification and bill CHI/PIN for the post-discharge tail. **This combines directly with Idea #1.**

The med school value is a 10. You'd be deploying an intervention with a 0.47 odds ratio behind it, with your own outcome data.

## 8. Phage library + host-range prediction

**Weighted score: 8.25 — highest moat on the list**

### Why the moat is real

Personalized phage therapy has a hard bottleneck that is *inherently* a data-and-inventory problem. In a cohort of drug-resistant mycobacterial disease, isolates from **200 culture-positive patients were screened and lytic phages were found for only 55** — because phage susceptibility varies enormously between isolates and relatively few active lytic phages are characterized.

The famous 2017 case required searching a collection of **~10,000 mycobacteriophages, 1,700 of them sequenced**, to find three usable phages for one patient ([GEN](https://genengnews.com/featured/phage-therapy-win-mycobacterium-infection-halted)). That collection was built largely by undergraduates: the **SEA-PHAGES program has engaged ~20,000 undergraduates at 130+ institutions, discovering more than 15,000 phages** ([AAU](https://www.aau.edu/node/26546)).

**A curated, sequenced, host-range-characterized phage library is a physical asset that cannot be downloaded.** Each new isolate-phage pairing improves your prediction model, which improves match rates, which attracts more isolates. That is a genuine data flywheel — the rarest thing on this list.

### Go vertical first, human later

The commercial proof is in agriculture, not medicine: **Phagos** raised a **€25M Series A (Oct 2025)** for its Alphagos platform, which analyzes full genomes of phages and bacteria to predict interactions, deployed across poultry, cattle, swine, and shrimp ([Vestbee](https://vestbee.com/blog/articles/phagos-raises-25-m)). **PhageLab** (Santiago) shows ~$44.5M total funding for livestock and poultry applications.

Veterinary and aquaculture markets have **no FDA drug approval pathway blocking you**, real customers with antibiotic-resistance problems, and the same underlying biology. Build the library and the prediction model there, generate revenue, and let the human compassionate-use pathway mature. Active human work continues — Vancouver Coastal Health opened an individual-patient M. abscessus phage study in January 2026 (NCT07228702).

### Risks

- **Hardest wet-lab requirement on the list.** Realistically requires a faculty partnership or an existing SEA-PHAGES program on your campus (check — there are 130+).
- Long timeline to human therapeutic revenue. Treat the vet market as the actual business, not a stepping stone you're embarrassed by.

## 9. Power resilience for electricity-dependent home medical equipment

**Weighted score: 8.05**

### The opening

**2.7 million Medicare beneficiaries rely on electricity-dependent durable medical equipment** — ventilators, oxygen concentrators, suction, infusion pumps — to live independently ([ASTHO](https://www.astho.org/topic/brief/when-the-power-fails-helping-life-support-equipment-users)). HHS built the **emPOWER Program** in 2013 with CMS to give jurisdictions de-identified counts of at-risk beneficiaries by area.

The data exists. The intervention mostly doesn't. PG&E runs a Portable Battery Program for Medical Baseline customers in California, with eligibility tied to recent PSPS events or repeated outages ([PG&E](https://www.pge.com/en/account/billing-and-assistance/financial-assistance/portable-battery-program.html)), and a companion program with CFILC offers batteries via grant, lease-to-own, or low-interest loan. **That's essentially one utility in one state.** A commercial power bank sized for this equipment retails near **$6,000** ([East County Magazine](https://www.eastcountymagazine.org/node/39724)).

Louisiana tried a genuinely creative workaround — **Power Outage Partners**, recruiting owners of standby generators to recharge neighbors' life-support batteries during outages, prioritizing in-home ventilator users ([ASTHO](https://www.astho.org/topic/brief/louisiana-recharge-neighbors-battery-powered-life-support-equipment)). It's a volunteer-matching model, which is exactly the kind of thing a student organization executes well.

### Model

Start as a local registry-and-matching nonprofit: identify electricity-dependent neighbors through DME suppliers and home health agencies, pair them with backup capacity (volunteer generators, donated/subsidized batteries, charging sites), and run drills before storm season. Then monetize two ways: (a) **utilities** — outage-related harm is a regulatory and liability problem, and PG&E's program proves they'll fund this; (b) **Medicare Advantage and Medicaid MCO plans** — a ventilator user losing power becomes an ER admission, which is the plan's cost.

### Why it's strong for you

Urgency is rising with grid instability and extreme weather, and the deaths are preventable, specific, and documentable. It's the kind of problem where a motivated undergraduate with a spreadsheet and a phone genuinely outperforms an institution. There's also a clean research output: no one has published good data on outage-related mortality in home ventilator users — I looked, and it isn't there. That gap is yours to fill.

### Risks

Fragmented buyers (every utility and plan is a separate sale), and the capital cost of batteries is real. Lead with the matching/registry layer, which costs almost nothing, and let hardware follow demonstrated demand.

## 10. Cascade genetic testing outreach

**Weighted score: 7.70**

### The opening

When someone tests positive for a hereditary cancer syndrome or familial hypercholesterolemia, their first-degree relatives each have a ~50% chance of carrying the same variant. **Uptake among those relatives is only 20–30%.** The standard of care is to hand the patient a letter and hope.

The 2026 **GIFT** platform trial (Michigan, *JCO*) tested contacting relatives **directly** instead of through the patient: of 948 relatives invited, **32% enrolled and 91.3% of enrollees ordered testing**. A smaller **Cascade Chatbot** QI study reported 51% of 96 relatives reached scheduled or completed a genetics appointment, with 73.5% of those completing testing ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20260416/virtual-tool-may-close-the-gap-in-cancer-genetic-testing-for-atrisk-relatives)).

The signal is clear and consistent: **removing the burden from the proband works.** And the tools proving it are academic research projects, not products. A randomized Cascade Genetic Testing Platform trial (NCT06927947, n=500) started September 2025.

### Model

Sell to genetics clinics and hereditary cancer programs as a managed outreach service: consented relative contact, education, scheduling, and testing logistics, with completion-rate reporting. Genetic counselors are scarce and expensive; this is work that doesn't require one but currently consumes one.

### Risks

Privacy is the central constraint — contacting relatives who are not your patients requires careful, jurisdiction-specific consent design. Get this reviewed by counsel early. Also, the evidence base is young, and the GIFT results show direct outreach helps but does not solve low uptake. Moat is moderate (6): the workflow is replicable, though clinic relationships and completion-rate benchmarks compound.

## Recommendation

**Run #1 or #4 as the engine, and #2 or #5 as the ambition.**

The pattern that works for a 19-year-old without capital is a services business with immediate revenue funding a defensible asset. Concretely:

- **Conservative, highest expected value:** #1 (CHI/PIN navigation) → layer in #7 (delirium companions) as a second contract type for the same trained workforce. Same labor pool, same hospital buyer, two revenue lines.
- **Highest ceiling:** #2 (Indigenous-language interpreting) — fund it with interpreting revenue, and the corpus becomes something nobody can replicate.
- **If you have wet-lab access:** #5 or #8. Check whether your campus runs SEA-PHAGES before dismissing #8.

The thing all ten share: none requires you to be a doctor first, and each produces exactly what medical schools say they're looking for — **evidence of service orientation, resilience, and systems thinking** — while also just being a real business.
