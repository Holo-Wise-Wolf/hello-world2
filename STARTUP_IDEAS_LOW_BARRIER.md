# 10 Startup Ideas — Reweighted for Low Barrier to Entry and Application Strength

**Revision of `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`, October 2026.** Same research base, new rubric. Moat weight removed entirely; barrier to entry and medical school application strength now carry 65% of the total between them.

This is not a cosmetic reshuffle. Dropping the moat criterion changed the ranking substantially, knocked two ideas out of the top ten, and — importantly — **re-qualified two ideas I had previously rejected**, one of which now ranks first.

---

## The new rubric

| Criterion | Old | **New** | What a 10 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low barrier to entry** | 25% | **35%** | Startable this semester for <$5k. No license, no lab, no FDA, no institutional permission you can't get with an email. First customer or patient inside 90 days. |
| **Med school application strength** | 20% | **30%** | Direct patient contact, a measurable outcome, a publishable result, and a story no other applicant can tell |
| **Medicine-related** | 10% | 10% | Touches patients, clinicians, or biology directly |
| **Interesting / non-mundane** | 10% | 10% | You'd want to explain it at a dinner party |
| **Novelty & innovation** | 15% | 15% | Nobody credible is already doing exactly this |
| ~~Future-proof / moat~~ | ~~20%~~ | **0%** | *Removed* |

**One caveat worth stating plainly:** removing moat weight is not the same as ignoring competition. A market with four funded competitors is still a bad place to start, because competition shows up in *profitability* and in how hard it is to land a first customer. So I kept competitive saturation as an input to the barrier score, just not as its own criterion. Where that distinction changed a verdict, I say so.

### What moved, and why

**Entered the list (both previously rejected):**

- **CHW-delivered hearing care → #1.** I cut this from v1 because it's a replication of a published model with no defensible moat. Under the new rubric that objection is weightless, and what's left is the single lowest-barrier idea I found with top-tier application value. Removing moat weight promoted it from "cut" to "first."
- **Barbershop-model hypertension → #10.** I rejected it in v1 for not being an undergraduate-led structure. On re-examination that was partly wrong — see #10 below — and its evidence base and application value are strong enough to carry a mediocre barrier score.

**Fell out of the top ten:**

- **Phage library + host-range prediction: #4 → #11 (7.65).** It had the highest moat score on the entire list (10). With that removed, a barrier score of 4 — it genuinely requires lab access and BSL containment — sinks it. Still the most scientifically interesting idea I researched. Just not a low-barrier one.
- **Cascade genetic testing: #10 → #12 (7.55).** Privacy and consent review for contacting non-patients is a real legal gate, and that's a barrier, not a moat.

**Biggest riser:** Delirium care companions, #8 → #4. It had the *worst* moat score in v1 (5) and was penalized hard for it. It also happens to be one of the easiest things on the list to actually start.

**Biggest faller:** Protein binder foundry, #3 → #8. Still excellent, still the highest-ceiling idea. But "you need wet-lab access and a faculty sponsor" is exactly what a barrier-weighted rubric punishes.

## 1. CHW-delivered hearing care for low-income older adults

**9.00 — lowest barrier on the list, paired with top application value**

### Why this is now first

The FDA's 2022 OTC rule means hearing aids no longer require an audiologist, a prescription, or a license to fit. The clinical question — does a trained non-professional fitting cheap devices actually help? — has been answered in a randomized trial, and the answer is yes.

Johns Hopkins built **HEARS**: trusted older adults are trained as community health workers and fit low-cost OTC devices for their peers in affordable housing. The randomized trial found improved communication function with benefits **similar in size to audiologist-fitted hearing aids** ([Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2022/12/program-that-trains-community-health-workers-to-deliver-hearing-care-shows-success-among-low-income-older-adults)), published in JAMA with the largest gains at three-month follow-up ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/patientengagement/news/366584702/How-Community-Health-Workers-Support-Hearing-Loss-Intervention)).

### The barrier is genuinely near zero

- **Devices cost $50–$220.** A Maryland county program cites an average OTC price of **$220**; a Chattanooga nonprofit reports **$50–$100 per device** ([Conduit Street](https://conduitstreet.mdcounties.org/2023/07/11/montgomery-county-partners-with-access-hears-free-hearing-aids-and-sound-amplifiers/); [Speech & Hearing Center](https://website.audiologydesign.com/speechhearing-com/hear-chattanooga/)).
- **No license required.** OTC by federal rule. The whole point of the HEARS model is that the fitter is a peer, not a professional.
- **The protocol is published.** You are not inventing a method; you are executing a validated one.
- **Your first site is a phone call.** Senior housing buildings, Area Agencies on Aging, and senior centers have populations with untreated hearing loss and no budget line for it.

Fifty devices and a training weekend is a real pilot for about $8,000 — and that's the unsubsidized version.

### Why it matters clinically (and why admissions committees will care)

Untreated hearing loss in older adults is not a quality-of-life footnote. It's associated with social isolation, depression, and accelerated cognitive decline, and it's among the largest *modifiable* dementia risk factors. You'd be delivering an intervention against that, in people's homes, with before/after communication-function data you collect yourself.

The application story writes itself and it is not a story about shadowing: *"I trained twelve residents of a subsidized senior building to fit hearing aids for their neighbors, and we measured the change."*

### Business model

Three plausible revenue paths, in order of difficulty:

1. **Grant and county-funded**, like Access HEARS and Montgomery County. Lowest friction, least scalable.
2. **Bill it.** This is the interesting one — it stacks directly onto Idea #3. Once your fitters hold state CHW certification, the service time is potentially billable under **CHI codes (G0019/G0022)** at ~$79/patient/month, or under state Medicaid CHW benefits. **26 states currently reimburse CHWs through Medicaid**, with 30-minute visit rates ranging from **$9.70 in Indiana to $55.25 in Arizona** ([IU comparative analysis](https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/IMPRS/article/download/27824/25246/55287)).
3. **Contract with Medicare Advantage plans**, which compete on supplemental hearing benefits and have no cheap delivery mechanism for them.

### Risks

- **Replication, not invention.** Access HEARS already exists and a HEARS Maryland trial (NCT07699146) plans 300 participants starting November 2026. You are not first. Under this rubric that's acceptable — but be honest about it in interviews, and differentiate on population or geography. The Korean-American adaptation (K-HEARS) shows the model travels across communities.
- **Cost data is thin.** The best per-participant figure I found — **~$1,183** — is from a Canadian program ([NB HSPP report](https://unb.ca/nbirdt/mektu/healthy-seniors-pilot-projects/_resources/pdf/c0021_hspp-end-of-project-report-synopsis_june2024.pdf)), not the U.S. trial. Build your own cost model; don't borrow that one.
- **Scope discipline.** You are fitting OTC amplification, not diagnosing. Sudden unilateral loss, pain, drainage, or vertigo get referred out, every time. Write the referral criteria before you fit anyone.

### First 90 days

1. Read the HEARS trial protocol and copy it.
2. Find one subsidized senior housing building with a willing property manager.
3. Recruit 3 resident fitters. Train them. Buy 25 devices.
4. Measure communication function at baseline and 3 months with a validated instrument (HHIE-S).
5. Pursue CHW certification in parallel so path #2 opens.

## 2. Indigenous-language medical interpreting

**8.85 — was #1, still excellent**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. The short version: **Mam, K'iche', and Q'anjob'al are each now among the 25 most common languages in U.S. immigration court**; UCSF Benioff Children's Oakland alone fields **1,000+ Mam interpreter requests per year** ([UCSF](https://pediatrics.ucsf.edu/news/breaking-down-barriers-vital-role-mam-speaking-healthcare-interpreters)). Section 1557 requires a **qualified human reviewer** for machine translation of critical content. Speech models fail badly on comparable low-resource languages — **26–70% character error rates** on isiXhosa clinical audio ([CL4Health 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/moar-dois/2025.cl4health-1.14)). Every funded competitor (No Barrier, Trava) covers Spanish/Mandarin/Arabic and skips these entirely.

**Why it only slipped one place:** its 10s on application strength, novelty, and interest are nearly untouchable. It lost ground only because the barrier is real — recruiting Mam interpreters requires community trust you have to earn in person, not capital you can raise. If you already have a connection to one of these communities, this is your idea and it should be #1 for you personally.

**What changed in the pitch:** without the moat criterion, the speech-corpus argument becomes secondary. Lead with the service, not the dataset. That's a better pitch to a hospital anyway.

## 3. Community health navigation billed under CHI/PIN codes

**8.70 — was #2**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. Medicare's **G0019/G0022** (Community Health Integration) and **G0023/G0024** (Principal Illness Navigation) pay roughly **$79/patient/month plus ~$49 per additional 30 minutes**, delivered by *auxiliary personnel under general supervision* — the physician need not be present ([Medical Economics](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/new-medicare-codes-could-transform-how-physicians-serve-their-most-vulnerable-patients-here-s-how-to-use-them)). National adoption in 2024 was minimal ([CodingIntel](https://codingintel.com/community-health-integration-chi-services/)). The 2026 rule broadened qualifying initiating visits and replaced "social determinants" with the wider **"upstream drivers."**

Precedent: **Rebecca Onie founded Health Leads as a Harvard sophomore in 1996** and won a MacArthur for it — running the same model on grants, because this billing code didn't exist yet.

**New detail from this round of research** — the certification barrier is real but mapped. **13 states require certification and supervision for CHW reimbursement.** Arkansas's Act 435 of 2025 established statewide standards, with one training provider listing **$3,500** for instruction, books, certification, BLS and Mental Health First Aid. Kansas offers an alternative **work-experience pathway: 800 hours over three years plus three recommendation letters**, and caps billing at 2 hours/day per member ([KDHE](https://www.kdhe.ks.gov/DocumentCenter/View/52038)). **Check your own state's pathway before anything else** — it's the difference between a 60-hour course and a $3,500 one.

**This is the hub idea.** Ideas #1 and #4 both plug into the same certified workforce and the same billing mechanism. If you build one thing, build this, then layer the others on.

## 4. Delirium-prevention care companions

**8.65 — was #8. Biggest riser on the list.**

### Why it jumped four places

In v1 I gave this the worst moat score on the board (5) and it dragged the total down. Moat was the *only* thing wrong with it. Strip that out and you're left with: a published protocol, extraordinary evidence, a volunteer-powered delivery model, and a hospital buyer with a budget line that's actively bleeding.

### The evidence

Hshieh et al. (2018, *Am J Geriatric Psychiatry*) pooled 14 HELP studies: **delirium incidence OR 0.47 (95% CI 0.37–0.59)**, **falls OR 0.58 (95% CI 0.35–0.95)**, and cost savings of **$1,600–$3,800 per patient** in 2018 dollars ([ASHA evidence map](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/e108b116-9454-ed11-813d-005056834e2b)). The 2015 *JAMA Internal Medicine* analysis across 4,267 patients found delirium **reduced 44%** and falls **64%** in randomized/matched trials.

**The reviews explicitly identify the volunteer role as valuable across studies.** This intervention was designed to run on people exactly like you.

### The money argument

Hospitals hate what they spend on 1:1 sitters: **~$2.1M/year** at one facility averaging 21 sitters per shift, **$3M/year unbudgeted** at a 900-bed hospital. Virtual sitting benchmarks the alternative at **$3.05/hour vs ~$26/hour** for in-person 1:1. One HELP evaluation documented sitter hours dropping 4,210 → 3,743 and falls 15 → 9, worth **~$48k on a single unit** — though note that sitter reduction was **not statistically significant (p=0.80)**, and one 2011-era study found a *net cost increase* of >$2 per patient day when sitter reduction let falls rise on med-surg floors.

Lead with delirium and falls. Treat sitter savings as the CFO's reason to say yes, not as your headline claim.

### Barrier: 9

A HELP program needs a unit-level champion (usually a geriatrician or the CNO), volunteer training, and a protocol that already exists in published form. No capital, no license, no technology. The hard part is getting one hospital to say yes — which is one well-prepared meeting.

### Application strength: 10

You'd be deploying an intervention with a 0.47 odds ratio behind it, collecting CAM screening data, and working directly with hospitalized older adults — plus a credible shot at a QI abstract. This is among the most legible "I did real clinical work" stories available to an undergraduate.

## 5. Power resilience for electricity-dependent home medical equipment

**8.55 — was #6**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. **2.7 million Medicare beneficiaries depend on electricity-dependent DME** — ventilators, oxygen concentrators, suction, infusion pumps ([ASTHO](https://www.astho.org/topic/brief/when-the-power-fails-helping-life-support-equipment-users)). HHS's **emPOWER** program already publishes the de-identified geographic data. Essentially one utility in one state does anything about it: PG&E's Portable Battery Program for Medical Baseline customers ([PG&E](https://www.pge.com/en/account/billing-and-assistance/financial-assistance/portable-battery-program.html)). A suitable battery retails near **$6,000**.

**Why it rose:** the registry-and-matching layer costs almost nothing to start. Louisiana's **Power Outage Partners** pilot — recruiting standby-generator owners to recharge neighbors' life-support batteries, prioritizing in-home ventilator users ([ASTHO](https://www.astho.org/topic/brief/louisiana-recharge-neighbors-battery-powered-life-support-equipment)) — is a volunteer-matching program, which is precisely the kind of thing a student organization executes well and a utility cannot.

**Research bonus:** nobody has published good data on outage-related mortality among home ventilator users. I looked; it isn't there. That gap is publishable.

## 6. Penicillin allergy de-labeling

**8.35 — was #5**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. **~90% of hospitalized patients labeled penicillin-allergic aren't**, and a cohort of **8,385 surgical patients showed 50% higher SSI risk** from the alternative prophylaxis those labels force ([MN DOH](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/onehealthabx/event/may53ba.pdf)). PEN-FAST risk-stratifies in minutes; Houston Methodist embedded it **directly in the EHR admission navigator for nursing staff system-wide**. I found **no commercial vendor** selling this.

**Economics remain genuinely contested** and you should present them that way: a 2025 UK evaluation found **no short-term savings** (£577–£2,329 per de-labeled patient), while U.S. and Portuguese models found savings of ~$657–$1,444 per inpatient. The reconciliation is target selection — screen pre-surgical, oncology, and ICU patients, not everyone.

**Barrier held at 7:** you need a hospital partner and pharmacist supervision for the oral challenge. That's a real gate, but a reachable one, and antimicrobial stewardship is already a Joint Commission requirement you'd be helping them meet.

## 7. Clinical research coordinator pipeline

**8.10 — was #9. Tied for lowest barrier on the list (10).**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. **CRC roles carry the most vacancies of any clinical research position** — more than nurses or PIs — with ~**7 job postings per experienced coordinator** and resignations **60% above 2020 levels** ([Advarra](https://resource.advarra.com/hubfs/eBook/Advarra%20Workforce%20Development%20Survey%20Report%20EB.pdf)). Sites are declining new studies. Meanwhile medical scribe headcount fell from **~100,000 in 2020 to ~50,000 in 2024** as ambient AI took over — the traditional pre-med entry job is disappearing just as an adjacent one is starving.

**Why it rose two places:** zero capital, zero regulatory gate, revenue in weeks. Train cohorts in GCP and HIPAA, place them as CRC-*assistants* (regulatory binders, source prep, pre-screening chart review, retention calls, data entry), keep the spread.

**Why it isn't higher:** novelty 6, interest 5. It is a staffing company and you should be clear-eyed that it is. Its job is to fund something more ambitious and put you — and dozens of peers — inside real trials.

## 8. De novo protein binder foundry

**7.85 — was #3. Biggest faller.**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. RFdiffusion and BindCraft are free; **Adaptyv tests a designed binder for ~$149 including two replicates**, with a 3-day turnaround option in private beta ([aiwiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/adaptyv_bio)). In their EGFR round, **130 designers submitted 1,131 binders** and the winner hit **1.21 nM**; a Nipah round saw a **9.3% hit rate across 1,200 designs, best affinity 0.37 nM**. Target orphan **research-use-only** reagents — no FDA pathway, immediate sales.

**Why it fell five places:** barrier score of 5. Anything past Adaptyv's screening service needs a wet lab, a faculty sponsor, and expression/scale-up work that is the actual engineering problem. Under a barrier-weighted rubric that's decisive.

**It remains the highest-ceiling idea on this list.** If you have lab access already, mentally move it back to #2 — the rubric is penalizing a constraint that may not apply to you.

## 9. Medical-frailty exemption infrastructure

**7.80 — was #7**

Full analysis in `STARTUP_IDEAS.md`. Medicaid work requirements take effect **January 1, 2027** under **CMS-2454-IFC** (June 1, 2026). **Self-attestation is usable only once starting in 2028**, after which enrollees must document their health status ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/the-medical-frailty-exemption-from-medicaid-work-requirements-key-takeaways-from-the-cms-interim-final-rule/)). Providers will be asked to certify frailty **with no reimbursement**, hitting safety-net and rural clinics hardest ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/issue-brief-medicaid-work-requirements-medical-frailty-exemption.pdf)). ACP is telling members to build EHR exemption templates; nobody sells one.

**Essentially unchanged in rank.** The policy volatility that made this risky under the old rubric is still risky under the new one, and the 24-month window remains its defining limitation. Ethical line to hold: you help eligible people keep coverage they're legally entitled to. Nothing else.

## 10. Barbershop-model community hypertension control

**7.75 — previously rejected. Re-qualified.**

### Why I was wrong to cut it

In v1 I rejected this as "not an undergraduate-led structure" because it requires a pharmacist prescribing under a collaborative practice agreement. That reasoning conflated *you need a licensed partner* with *you can't build it*. Plenty of viable organizations are built around a clinical partner; the student runs recruitment, logistics, retention, and measurement, and the pharmacist prescribes. Under a rubric that rewards application strength at 30%, cutting this was a mistake.

### The evidence is among the strongest in community health

The Los Angeles Barbershop Blood Pressure Study randomized **52 barbershops**. Barbers promoted follow-up with pharmacists who prescribed under collaborative practice agreements with patrons' primary care providers. Results: **systolic BP reduction of 21.6 mmHg versus control, sustained through 12 months** ([TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/barbershop-intervention-high-bp-has-lasting-effects)). At six months, **63.6% of the intervention group reached <130/80 versus 11.7% of controls**, with **95% cohort retention**.

A 21.6 mmHg sustained systolic reduction is a larger effect than most antihypertensive drugs produce in trials. This is a genuinely remarkable result.

### Economics

Cost-effectiveness analyses put it at **$42,717 per QALY gained**, improving to **$17,162 per QALY with generic-only prescribing**. To be cost-neutral, the program would need to cost **~$237 per person per year excluding medications**. That's a real number you can build a budget against, and it's well within what a Medicaid MCO or an ACO would pay for hypertension control in a population they're measured on.

### Barrier: 6 — the honest constraint

You need a pharmacist with prescriptive authority under a CPA, and **adoption of the physician/pharmacist collaborative practice model has been slow in many states**. CPA scope varies significantly by state, and this is the first thing to check — before recruiting a single barbershop. A Nashville feasibility study (NCT04232124) is running a smaller version of the model, which is a useful template for scope.

If your state has permissive CPA rules and a pharmacy school nearby, the barrier drops to about an 8 and this idea jumps to the top five.

### Novelty: 5 — the honest weakness

This is a well-known, widely admired model. You would be replicating it, not inventing it. Pick an unserved population or geography, or adapt the channel (the "barbershop" is really "a trusted community space where the target population already goes voluntarily and repeatedly" — that generalizes to salons, churches, union halls, taquerias).

## What I'd actually do under this rubric

**Start with #1 (hearing care) or #4 (delirium companions) this semester.** Both are startable in under 90 days with under $10k, both have published protocols you can execute rather than invent, and both put you in sustained direct contact with older patients while generating your own outcome data.

**Then connect them through #3.** Get CHW-certified in your state. The same certified workforce can fit hearing aids (#1), run HELP protocols (#4), and bill CHI/PIN (#3). That's one organization with three services and one credentialing pipeline — far stronger than three separate attempts, and the billing code turns a volunteer program into something that sustains itself.

**If you have lab access, ignore the rubric on #8.** The barrier score that knocked the binder foundry from #3 to #8 is measuring a constraint that may simply not apply to you. Same for #11 (phage) if your campus runs SEA-PHAGES — check, because 130+ institutions do.

The reweighting mostly rewarded one thing: **ideas where the method is already published and your job is execution and measurement, not invention.** That is a genuinely good fit for an undergraduate, and it's also — not coincidentally — what produces the clearest evidence of competence on an application.

## Verification notes

Carried forward from v1, plus new items:

- CHI/PIN payment rates (~$79/$49) come from secondary commentary — verify in the current CMS Physician Fee Schedule.
- CHW Medicaid reimbursement counts (26 states covering, 13 requiring certification) come from a single comparative analysis with no visible publication date. Check your own state's Medicaid agency directly.
- OTC hearing device prices ($50–$220) are local program figures, not national data. The **~$1,183 per-participant** cost is from a Canadian program, not the U.S. HEARS trial.
- Barbershop trial cost-effectiveness figures date from the original trial period and are not 2025 replication costs. **State CPA law is the binding constraint — check it first.**
- Hospital sitter-cost figures are largely self-reported; treat as directional. The sitter-hour reduction in the cited HELP evaluation was not statistically significant.
- The 2025 UK penicillin de-labeling economic evaluation contradicts earlier U.S. and Portuguese cost-savings models. The disagreement is genuine and unresolved.
- School vision screening evidence is mixed across trials (positive in Baltimore 2021, null in Canada 2024).
