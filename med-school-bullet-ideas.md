# 10 High-Leverage Med School Application Ideas (AI-Assisted, Low-to-Moderate Effort)

*Researched October 2026. Numbers in [brackets] in the sample bullets are **targets**. Replace them with your real results; the bullet only works if it's true.*

---

## What the research says admissions committees reward

1. **Competencies, not activities.** The AAMC scores applicants against 17 "Premed Competencies": 11 professional (service orientation, empathy, teamwork, reliability, resilience, oral communication, ethical responsibility, commitment to learning, interpersonal skills, plus two cultural ones that the AAMC renamed in 2026 to "understanding others" and "self-awareness"), 4 thinking and reasoning (critical thinking, quantitative reasoning, scientific inquiry, written communication), and 2 science. A good bullet shows evidence for several of these at once.
2. **Depth beats breadth.** Committees favor sustained commitment and measurable impact over long lists. Labels like "founder" don't impress on their own; what counts is your specific role and what changed because of you.
3. **Format constraints.** AMCAS allows 15 experiences with 700 characters each. Three can be marked "Most Meaningful", which adds 1,325 characters, and an experience you only plan to do can't be one of the three. Every entry needs a contact person, so each idea below has a natural verifier.
4. **AI rules.** AMCAS allows AI for brainstorming, editing and organizing, but you certify that the final application reflects your own work and experiences. Interviewers will probe every entry. The test for each idea: *can you talk about it for 10 minutes, including what went wrong and what you personally did?*

**Design principle used to pick these 10:** in each one, AI does most of the grunt work (drafting, coding, scraping, translating, formatting), you do the human parts (relationships, judgment, patient contact, verification), and the result is a **quantified outcome with a named verifier**.

## 1. Plain-language patient-materials overhaul

**Problem.** A 2024 *JAMA Otolaryngology* systematic review of 1,124 patient-education materials found average reading levels from grade 8.8 to 14.8, and **none** of the 17 studies had a mean at or below the AMA/NIH-recommended 6th grade. More than a third of patients had inadequate health literacy. Since the Cures Act, patients also see lab results in their portal, often before a clinician has explained them.

**What you do.**
- Partner with a free clinic, FQHC, or hospital department and collect their handouts.
- AI rewrites each one to grade 6 or below and computes readability scores (Flesch-Kincaid, SMOG).
- You score each handout before and after with AHRQ's **PEMAT** (a 70% score is the usual "good" threshold).
- A clinician verifies accuracy, then you test the new versions with [10] patients using teach-back.
- **Variant:** a library of plain-language "what your lab result means" sheets for portal users.

**Sample bullet.**
> Rewrote [38] patient-education handouts for [Westside Free Clinic] using an AI-assisted, physician-verified workflow, lowering average reading level from grade [11.2] to [5.8] and raising PEMAT understandability from [54%] to [86%]; adopted clinic-wide ([~2,000] visits/yr) and presented as a QI poster at [SSRFC / state conference].

**Why it lands.** It shows written communication, service orientation and understanding others, with before-and-after numbers. It also turns into a QI poster easily (the *Journal of Student-Run Clinics* publishes exactly this kind of work).

**Watch-outs.** Never publish AI-rewritten medical content without clinician sign-off. Keep the version history as proof of the work.

## 2. Free-clinic medication-access engine (Patient Assistance Programs + low-cost pharmacies)

**Problem.** Uninsured patients skip medications they can't afford. Drug manufacturers run Patient Assistance Programs (PAPs) that give medications free, but every company has different eligibility rules and paperwork, so applications are tedious. Published examples of what volunteer-run PAP work can secure:
- One student-run clinic: $222,563 in medications for 61 patients.
- One hospital program: about $2.06M in no-cost fills.

Demand is rising. Enhanced ACA subsidies expired after 2025, Marketplace enrollment fell from 21.8M to 19.2M, about 9% of 2025 enrollees are now uninsured, and free clinics are reporting record visit numbers.

**What you do.**
- AI builds the clinic a **PAP eligibility matrix** covering [40] manufacturers (income limits, required documents, renewal dates) and reusable form templates.
- It also builds a cash-price comparison against discount programs (Cost Plus Drugs, the $4 generic lists, GoodRx).
- You do the human work: meet patients, collect documents, follow up with drug companies, and track renewals.

**Sample bullet.**
> Built a patient-assistance-program tracker covering [40] manufacturer programs for [Clinic]; personally enrolled [72] uninsured patients in free or low-cost medications worth an estimated $[180,000]/yr and eliminated missed renewals.

**Why it lands.** It combines patient contact, a dollar figure and a clear story about real people's barriers. The clinic director makes a strong contact and letter writer.

**Watch-outs.** **Never put patient information into a consumer AI tool.** Use AI only to build templates and the matrix; patient data stays in the clinic's own systems.

## 3. "Secret shopper" access audit → first-author publication

**Problem.** Many things are legal or available "on paper" but hard to get in practice, and the way to measure that is a simulated-patient audit. Students have run these before: two first-year MPH students made more than 760 Medicaid calls in Seattle. Recent findings show the gaps are real and the questions are publishable:
- **OTC naloxone:** a national audit of 1,108 pharmacies found only 61.4% had it the same day, and about 1 in 5 of those with it in stock wrongly said a prescription was needed.
- **Opill (OTC birth control):** uptake is low, and a funded Pennsylvania secret-shopper study has *no results yet*, so this is open territory in most states.
- **Language access:** Spanish-speaking callers face more transfers, hang-ups and longer waits when booking primary care.
- **Kratom/7-OH:** the FDA recommended scheduling 7-OH in July 2025 and the DEA moved toward temporary scheduling in 2026, so whether stores still sell it is an untracked, timely question.

**What you do.**
- Find a faculty mentor; health-policy, pharmacy, family-medicine and public-health faculty all run these.
- AI drafts the one-page pitch, protocol, call script, IRB application, sampling frame (from public pharmacy or provider lists), the R/Python analysis and the first manuscript draft.
- You make the calls, interpret the results and revise the manuscript.

**Sample bullet.**
> Designed and led an IRB-approved simulated-patient audit of [312] [Ohio] pharmacies' over-the-counter contraception access; found only [38%] stocked Opill on open shelves and [1 in 5] staff gave inaccurate information; first-author research letter submitted to [journal]; findings presented to [state Board of Pharmacy].

**Why it lands.** Original research, a first-authorship, and a health-equity framing that's easy to explain in an interview. Research-heavy schools especially value it.

**Watch-outs.** Studies that involve deception need IRB review, even if the review ends in an exemption. Keep calls short and never book real appointment slots. Avoid predatory journals.

## 4. "Ask Smarter": community workshops on using AI safely for health questions

**Problem.**
- A KFF poll (Feb–Mar 2026) found that **32% of U.S. adults used AI chatbots for health information in the past year**, and 1 in 5 of them did so because care was hard to access or afford.
- Earlier KFF data (2024) found only about 29% *trust* chatbot health information.
- Accuracy varies widely: one meta-analysis of early studies estimated 56% overall.
- No published evaluation exists of library or senior-center workshops that teach adults to use AI safely for health questions. A Louisiana grant project is only now building a framework, so this is still open territory.

**What you do.**
- Build a 60-minute curriculum, with AI drafting the slides and exercises:
  - What AI is good for: decoding jargon, preparing questions for a visit.
  - What it isn't: dosing, emergencies, replacing care.
  - Never paste in your identifiers.
  - How to cross-check with MedlinePlus.
  - Red-flag symptoms that need a real clinician.
- Partner with public libraries or senior centers and run pre- and post-surveys.
- If you want to publish, get an IRB determination before the first session.

**Sample bullet.**
> Created and taught "Ask Smarter," a 60-minute workshop on using AI chatbots safely for health questions; delivered [9] sessions at [County Library] to [150] adults, mostly 60+; participants able to identify unsafe AI advice rose from [31%] to [84%]; curriculum adopted across [library system].

**Why it lands.** It's very timely: interviewers ask about AI in medicine constantly, and this shows *judgment* about AI rather than hype. It also covers oral communication and service orientation, it's fast, and it's AI-native in an ethical way.

**Watch-outs.** Make it educational, not medical advice. Track your outcome numbers honestly.

## 5. Campus naloxone-in-AED-cabinet campaign

**Problem.** Overdoses happen on and near campuses, and naloxone often isn't reachable in time. Precedents show students can fix this:
- McMaster students installed 32 public naloxone kits using data-driven placement.
- A University of Arizona student group got naloxone placed in **existing AED cabinets** campus-wide.
- Several states give out kits and cabinets free (for example, Arkansas funded 50 NaloxBox stations at one university).

**What you do.**
- Check what your campus already has first.
- AI drafts the proposal to student affairs and risk management, the state Good Samaritan law summary and the free-supply applications, and builds the placement map.
- You get the approvals, recruit allies and run trainings.
- **If your campus is already covered,** pivot to nearby bars, libraries, shelters or transit hubs.

**Sample bullet.**
> Led a student campaign that placed [36] naloxone emergency kits in existing AED cabinets across [University], securing free supply through [State] program; trained [280] students and staff in overdose recognition and response.

**Why it lands.** Leadership, advocacy, persistence through bureaucracy (resilience) and a visible public-health result.

## 6. Targeted donor drives for underrepresented patients (marrow registry + sickle-cell blood)

**Problem.**
- Black patients have about a **29%** chance of finding a fully matched (8/8) unrelated donor on the NMDP registry, versus about 79% for White patients.
- Black donors are under 4% of Red Cross donors, while sickle cell patients do best with closely antigen-matched blood.
- The Red Cross Sickle Cell Initiative runs through December 2027.

**What you do.**
- Partner with NMDP and the Red Cross, which handle all the medical logistics, plus a Black Student Union, a church, or an HBCU-alumni or cultural group.
- AI drafts culturally tailored outreach materials and tracks sign-ups.
- Note: only people aged 18–40 can join the NMDP registry.

**Sample bullet.**
> Organized [3] culturally tailored donor drives with NMDP and the American Red Cross in partnership with [Black Student Union], registering [240] new stem-cell donors ([65%] from underrepresented groups) and collecting [130] units for the Sickle Cell Initiative.

**Why it lands.** This is the lowest-effort idea on the list, with clear numbers and an equity framing. The *targeting* is what separates it from a generic blood drive.

## 7. Medicaid work-requirement navigator toolkit (deadline: Jan 1, 2027)

**Problem.**
- Medicaid expansion adults in 43 states must meet new work and community-engagement requirements by **January 1, 2027**. Some states started earlier (Nebraska in 2026), and most will re-verify every six months.
- In Arkansas's 2018 trial, about 17,000–18,000 people lost coverage, and a third of the target group hadn't even heard of the rules. Researchers attributed most of the losses to confusion and red tape rather than people not working.
- On top of this, ACA subsidy losses are pushing more people into the safety net.

**What you do.**
- Partner with a legal-aid organization, an FQHC or a navigator group.
- AI drafts plain-language, multilingual explainers on who's exempt, what counts as qualifying hours and how to report.
- The partner organization's attorneys or staff review everything.
- You staff help sessions under the partner's supervision.

**Sample bullet.**
> Partnered with [Legal Aid of X] to create attorney-reviewed, plain-language guides to Medicaid's new work requirements in [4] languages and staffed [10] community help sessions, helping [85] enrollees document work hours or exemptions ahead of the January 2027 rollout.

**Why it lands.** Social determinants of health, a timely policy issue, service and written communication. It's also a natural "Community Health Advocacy" entry.

**Watch-outs.** Stay informational and work under the partner's supervision; don't give individualized legal advice. Frame it neutrally: you're helping eligible people keep coverage.

## 8. AI-translation safety project (if you're bilingual)

**Problem.** Machine translation of medical instructions is "almost good", which is dangerous:
- **Spanish and Chinese:** GPT-4 was 95–97% accurate sentence by sentence, but 16–24% of full instruction sets contained at least one error (Russian: 56%).
- **Haitian Creole:** ChatGPT had about 33% potentially clinically significant errors, versus 8% for professional translators.
- **Somali and Armenian:** a 2026 study rated ChatGPT-4o translations worst for these digitally underrepresented languages.
- Most less-common languages haven't been studied at all.

**What you do.**
- **Research version:** with a mentor, evaluate AI translation (or AI answers to common health questions) in *your* language against professional translation, graded by bilingual clinicians.
- **Service version:** run an "AI drafts, certified human verifies" pipeline for a clinic's materials.
- **Bonus:** get certified as a medical interpreter (about a 40-hour course) and volunteer as one, which makes the work clinical.

**Sample bullet.**
> Evaluated AI translation of [50] discharge-instruction sets into [Vietnamese] with [3] bilingual physicians; [26%] contained ≥1 clinically significant error; presented at [conference]; findings shaped [Clinic]'s policy requiring human review of translated patient materials.

**Why it lands.** It uses your language skills as the thing that makes you uniquely qualified, adds scientific inquiry, and avoids the crowded "ChatGPT answers questions about X" genre by asking a question few people *can* study.

## 9. Public-dataset research letter (AI writes the code; you own the science)

**Problem / opportunity.**
- Free, de-identified public datasets generally aren't human-subjects research, so you usually don't need an IRB.
- Examples: FDA **FAERS** (drug adverse events), **MAUDE** (device problems), CDC **WONDER** (mortality, overdoses), **NHANES**, **BRFSS**, CMS **Open Payments**.
- AI makes data cleaning and analysis code fast.
- Timely angles:
  - Adverse events tied to concentrated 7-OH/kratom products (poison-center calls roughly doubled in 2025).
  - Compounded GLP-1 adverse events.
  - Device complaints for new over-the-counter glucose monitors.

**What you do.** Pick one sharp question, find a mentor who will check your methods, and write a 600–1,000-word research letter. Aim for a poster at a regional or national meeting at the same time.

**Sample bullet.**
> First author, research letter analyzing [4,800] FDA adverse-event reports involving [concentrated 7-OH] products, 2019–2026; performed all analyses in R (AI-assisted coding, mentor-verified); presented at [conference]; manuscript under review at [journal].

**Why it lands.** Scientific inquiry, quantitative reasoning and a publication.

**Watch-outs.** You *must* understand every line of the analysis, because interviewers ask. Avoid predatory journals, and disclose AI use as the journal requires.

## 10. Grant writing for a resource-strapped free clinic

**Problem.** There are about 1,400 free and charitable clinics in the U.S., 45% of them run on **$250K a year or less**, and patient visits are hitting records. Grant writing is the work that's most missing, and the work AI speeds up most.

**What you do.**
- Ask the director what they'd fund with $[25K].
- AI helps you find funders (local community foundations, hospital community-benefit programs, pharma and corporate giving) and draft the letter of inquiry, narrative and budget.
- You gather the clinic data and the director approves every submission.

**Sample bullet.**
> Co-wrote [5] grant proposals for [Free Clinic], securing $[42,000] to launch a diabetes-supply program serving [110] uninsured patients.

**Why it lands.** A dollar figure, service orientation, teamwork, and a director who can speak to your reliability.

**Watch-outs.** Check each funder's AI policy. NIH won't treat applications "substantially developed by AI" as original, though small private foundations usually have no such rule. Even "submitted $[120K] in proposals" is a fine bullet if no awards come in before you apply.

## The multiplier: stack ideas in ONE organization

The biggest risk with "low-effort" projects is that they read as **thin and scattered**. The fix is to run 2–4 of these at the **same anchor organization**, for example a free clinic or FQHC:

- **#1** (handouts) and **#2** (medication access) are the core.
- Add **#10** (grants), and **#7** (Medicaid) if the clinic sees Medicaid patients.

Over 6–9 months this becomes one deep, longitudinal **"Most Meaningful"** experience with one strong letter writer, instead of four disconnected entries. Run one research idea (**#3**, **#8**, or **#9**) alongside it for scientific inquiry, and one community-facing idea (**#4**, **#5**, or **#6**) for oral communication and leadership.

## Timeline (from mid-October 2026)

If you're applying in the cycle that opens **May 2027**, you have about 7 months.

- **Start now:**
  - **#7**, which is deadline-driven.
  - **#3** and **#9**: mentors and IRBs are slow.
  - **#2**, so you build up hours.
- **Fast wins by spring:** **#1**, **#4**, **#6**, **#10**.
- **After you submit:** research that's "submitted" or "under review" still counts. Send update letters or mention it in secondaries when it's accepted.

## Considered and demoted (and why)

- **"We tested ChatGPT's answers about [condition]" papers:** an extremely crowded genre that's already been pooled in meta-analyses. Only worth doing with a novel angle, such as low-resource languages (#8).
- **A patient-facing symptom checker or medical chatbot:** patient-safety and liability problems, and interviewers will grill you on them.
- **An MCAT or premed app or tutoring business:** reads as self-serving padding unless it clearly serves disadvantaged students.
- **Wikipedia medical editing:** since **March 2026**, English Wikipedia's guideline bars LLM-generated or LLM-rewritten article text, so the AI shortcut is gone. It's still a nice entry if you write it yourself and track pageviews.
- **Medical-debt abolition fundraiser** (about $1 erases $100): great optics, but two randomized trials (*QJE* 2025) found no average effect on recipients' health or financial outcomes. Fine as a side activity; don't make it your headline.
- **Building your own SMS-reminder system for a clinic:** evidence is mixed (a significant drop at one student-run clinic, no change at a rural one) and it raises HIPAA questions. Helping a clinic adopt an existing platform such as CareMessage is the better version.
- **"Founded a nonprofit" for its own sake:** committees judge your role and the impact, not the label.

## Honorable mentions (low effort, no AI needed)

- **Stop the Bleed instructor** (non-healthcare people can qualify after the course; teach classes at schools and community groups).
- **Certified medical interpreter** (if bilingual; makes free-clinic work clinical).
- **Community-resource directory audit** for a clinic's referral list (in one Georgia study, 75% of provider-directory listings had errors and 15% of phone numbers were wrong).
- **Crisis Text Line** (remote, flexible, patient-facing in spirit).

## Ground rules

1. Every number on your application must be real. Use the bracketed numbers as targets, not claims.
2. Keep patient information out of consumer AI tools.
3. Anything patient-facing needs a qualified reviewer (clinician, attorney, certified translator).
4. Disclose AI use wherever journals or funders require it.
5. AI may help edit your AMCAS descriptions, but they must reflect your own work.
